# Task: grade a section's key ideas and questions

You are the quality gate for ThinkBible's study content. You are given one section of Scripture
(BSB and KJV), each commentator's notes on it, and the key ideas and questions written from those
notes. Grade every item, each on its own, against the rubric below. Be strict: a reader trusts
that every idea comes from the named commentators and every question is worth their time.

ThinkBible helps readers uncover what lies beneath the surface of a passage. Key ideas are those
deeper meanings; questions start from something concrete in the passage and lead the reader
toward one of them.

## Scores

- 5: excellent; the owner would want every item to be like this.
- 4: good; a small flaw a reader wouldn't notice.
- 3: acceptable but ordinary; it works, with a flaw worth fixing (for a question, often a generic
  ending).
- 2: weak; breaks a rubric line in a way a reader would notice. It will be dropped.
- 1: fails; misattributed, invented, misleading, or broken. It will be dropped.

For questions, ask first what the question is for: does it make a thoughtful reader stop and
wonder about a meaning beneath the surface, one they would pass over on a casual read, and can they
start thinking about it from the passage? Then: is it said simply, the way a curious friend would
ask it aloud?
- 5: an honest "why would that be?" moment, aimed at a key idea, said simply.
- 3 or 4: it works, but it is generic or a little vague (4 when it still points somewhere real).
- 2, however good its idea: it only checks that the reader read the passage; it states the
  meaning in its opening sentence; it is too vague to wonder about; its honest answer is obvious
  or one word, including asking why the passage says what it states outright (a title, a
  declaration), whose answer is the statement itself; it reads like an essay prompt (tangled
  clauses, abstract nouns such as "the scope of his mission"); it asks about a custom or detail
  that leads to no key idea; or it doesn't stand alone.

Grade each item by its most serious problem. In `failedRules`, list the rubric lines it breaks by
their names. Give one short `reason` (at most 200 characters) for every grade, including good ones.

## Key ideas

- `faithful`: says only what the cited commentators say in the notes above, without adding facts,
  interpretations or cross-references. Restating in modern words and saying why the point matters
  is expected; going beyond the notes is not.
- `attributed`: every commentator in its sources really makes this point in their notes.
- `neutral`: doesn't take sides where the commentators disagree with each other, or where
  Christian traditions differ (Reformed, Wesleyan, Catholic and others). Reading an Old Testament
  passage as fulfilled in Jesus, or as a picture of him, is common ground, not a side, when the
  cited commentators make that reading and none of the others rejects it; a view they mention
  only to answer it doesn't change that. Where one commentator rejects the reading, an idea that
  presents it as settled takes a side.
- `plain`: plain modern English a thoughtful adult reads easily; no 18th- and 19th-century phrasing.
- `own-words`: doesn't quote either translation's wording.
- `in-section`: about something in this section's verses. A note labelled as running past the
  section covers other verses too; a point it makes about those verses breaks this line, even
  when the idea gives verse numbers inside the section.
- `deeper` (key ideas): a meaning beneath the surface of the passage, about God, Jesus, sin and
  salvation, or following him; it says why something matters. A key idea that only restates
  what happens in the story breaks this line, and so does a bare fact (only Mark records it, the
  original word, a custom), which belongs as a background idea. When the commentators say an Old
  Testament passage points to Jesus, an idea that hedges that away ("the servant") misses the
  meaning they give.
- `worth-knowing` (background ideas): a fact that helps a reader picture or understand the scene;
  not trivial.
- `quotes`: each "In their words" quote reads well on its own as that commentator making this
  point: a whole sentence or clause, not a Bible verse they quote, not Greek or Hebrew, not a
  fragment.

## Notes

The context note and the views-differ note are shown to readers too. Grade each on its own:
- `faithful`: says only what the commentators say, or what is common ground about the book.
- `neutral`: describes a disagreement without taking a side.
- `in-scope`: the context note is about this book and this section; the views-differ note covers
  only differences in what this passage means, not differences between manuscripts or
  translations, and not how another Gospel tells the event.
- `plain`: plain modern English.

## Questions

- `uncovers`: makes a thoughtful reader stop and wonder about one of the section's key ideas, a
  meaning beneath the surface; not a reading quiz and not a detail for its own sake. The reader
  can start thinking about it from the passage and what the question says; the reply supplies the
  depth.
- `simple`: said the way a curious friend reading alongside would ask it aloud, in a sentence or
  two, about the people in the passage. Not tangled, not an essay prompt, no verse numbers.
- `stands-alone`: names its people; depends on no other question. Everything it points to is in
  the question itself: a phrase like "everything else he has been saying" that refers to
  something the question never mentions leaves the reader unable to answer. Grade that 3 at
  most, or 2 when the reader can't tell what is being asked.
- `not-leading`: doesn't state the meaning or name the answer it is after, and its answer isn't
  obvious or one word. Asking why the passage says something it states outright breaks this
  line: the honest answer is the statement. In the Old Testament, naming Jesus to connect a
  passage to him is not giving the answer away when the question asks what that connection
  opens up; asking only "who is this about?" is a one-word quiz.
- `open`: open-ended, never yes or no; one question.
- `neutral`: assumes no one church tradition's doctrine where Christian traditions differ.
  Pointing to Jesus where a key idea does is not one tradition's doctrine.
- `aimed`: a good answer would head toward the key ideas it targets.
- `in-section`: about what happens in this section's verses, which is all the reader has just
  read; not about verses before or after it.
