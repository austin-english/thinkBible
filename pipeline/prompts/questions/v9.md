# Task: guiding questions for one section

You are given one section of Scripture in two translations (BSB and KJV), each commentator's
notes on it, and the key ideas drawn from those notes, each with its ID. Write up to 8 candidate
questions, in the order a reader should meet them. A grader scores every one, and the three best
are kept, in your order, so give real options: different ideas and different ways in, each one a
question you would be glad to see kept. Follow the question guide below.

## What a question is for

A question guides the reader into the passage's meaning before they see what the commentators
saw. It is never a reading quiz. Aim at the big picture: the main ideas the commentators draw
from the passage, not a minor detail for its own sake. A detail is a good way in when it opens
one of those ideas (a surprising word, a tension, an unusual order of events, a link to what came
before). Point the reader to it and ask a question that makes them want to think it through.

## The commentators' notes are for you, not the reader

The reader has only the passage in front of them. Use the notes to find what is worth asking and
the angle to ask from: when a commentator explains why a detail matters, ask about that detail in
a way that leads a thoughtful reader toward the point, without stating it. Never put the
commentators' conclusions, interpretations or facts from their notes into the question.

## Rules

- **Ask about the people, in plain words.** Name who acts and use ordinary verbs: "Why might
  Jesus silence it?", not "Why might a true confession be rejected?". The question is one simple
  clause. Never let an abstract noun stand in for what happened: no "the distinction", "the
  affirmation", "a true confession", "the nature of", "that framing", "what that detail adds".
- **Read it aloud.** Ask it the way a friend reading alongside would ask it out loud. If it sounds
  like an essay prompt, say it more simply.
- **Specific.** The question names what to think about: why someone acts as they do, why this
  order, what something cost them, what changes for the people in the scene. A question that
  could be asked of any passage ("What do you make of that detail?") is too vague.
- **The opening sentence says what happens, not what it means.** Point to what the passage shows
  (who does what, the words used, the order of events). The point the commentators draw stays
  out: that is what the reader should find.
- **Background only when the reader needs it to answer**, stated plainly as fact the passage
  assumes ("Lepers had to live apart from others"). Never the commentators' reading of it, and
  nothing that needs another Gospel ("Only Mark mentions…").
- **Worth answering.** It holds a real puzzle, tension or surprise, so a thoughtful reader wants to
  think it through. An answer that is obvious from the opening sentence makes a weak question.
- Never ask the reader to repeat what the text says: no "What does verse 6 say…", "What two
  things…", "How does John describe…", "Who…", "Where…".
- Aim at a key idea (`targets`: the idea IDs) without giving it away.
- Answerable by thinking about the passage alone.
- Open-ended, one question, ending with "?". At most 30 words in all; most good questions need 15
  to 25. One opening sentence and one question; no second clause tacked on ("and what does that
  say about…").
- Not leading, and assuming no one tradition's doctrine.
- Quoting: if you put a word or phrase in quotation marks, use single quotation marks ('like
  this'), never double ones. A double quotation mark ends the answer's text field early.
- Doesn't quote wording found in only one of the two translations. Describe it, or use words both
  share. To point to a verse, write `{{ref:C:V}}` or `{{ref:C:V-V}}`.

## Weak questions and better ones (Mark 1-2)

- Abstract nouns instead of the people: "The unclean spirit says true things about Jesus, yet
  Jesus silences it rather than allowing those words to stand. Why might a true confession still
  be rejected?"
  Plain: "The spirit knows exactly who Jesus is, but Jesus tells it to be quiet. Why might Jesus
  silence it?"
- An essay prompt: "John distinguishes his water baptism from what the one coming after him will
  bring. What does that distinction say about the limits of what John's ministry can accomplish?"
  Plain: "John says his baptism is with water, but the one coming will baptize with the Spirit.
  What is John telling the crowd about Jesus?"
- Strung-out clauses: "Why might it matter that the diseases varied rather than Jesus being known
  for curing one kind of illness?"
  Plain: "People bring Jesus every kind of sickness, and he heals them. What does that say about
  who can come to him?"
- Vague, nearly restates the idea: "Jesus calls people to both repent and believe. Why might both
  matter, rather than just one or the other?"
  Plain and specific: "Jesus calls people to repent and to believe. What would be missing if
  someone did only one?"
- Opening sentence states the point: "Jesus heals the man visibly to prove he can forgive sins.
  Why might a physical miracle be needed to back that claim?"
  What happens: "Jesus asks whether it is easier to forgive sins or to heal. Why might he ask that
  before healing the man?"
- Question hands over the answer: "Jesus calls working fishermen rather than religious leaders.
  What might that choice say about where the power of their ministry would come from?"
  Leaves it to the reader: "Jesus calls fishermen at their nets, not teachers or priests. Why
  might he begin with men like these?"
- Pushes the commentators' reading: "Leprosy was contagious and incurable. What might that double
  feature suggest about what it represents in this passage?"
  Plain background, then the passage: "Lepers had to live apart and keep others away. Why might it
  matter that Jesus touched this man?"

## Order

The reader meets one question at a time. The first comes on their first read; each next one a
week later, when they come back to the section. So:
1. First, the question that opens the passage's main idea, usually about the passage as a whole.
2. Then questions on the other key ideas, each aimed at a different idea than the ones before it.
3. A question connecting the passage to the rest of Scripture or to the reader's own life fits
   well later in the list.

Questions are not ranked by difficulty: every one should be worth thinking about on its own.

## What to record

- `type`: `meaning` (what the passage, or something in it, means), `application` (connecting it
  to the rest of Scripture or to life), or `noticing` (points at a detail and asks what the
  reader makes of it; use only when the detail opens a key idea).
- `verseStart`, `verseEnd`: the verses the question is about, inside the section. Use the whole
  section for a question about the passage as a whole.
