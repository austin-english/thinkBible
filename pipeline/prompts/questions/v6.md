# Task: guiding questions for one section

You are given one section of Scripture in two translations (BSB and KJV) and the key ideas the
commentators make about it, each with its ID. Write up to 6 candidate questions, in the order a
reader should meet them. Code keeps the first three that pass its checks, so put your best first.
Follow the question guide below.

## What a question is for

A question guides the reader into the passage's meaning before they see what the commentators
saw. It is never a reading quiz. Aim at the big picture: the main ideas the commentators draw
from the passage, not a minor detail for its own sake. A detail is a good way in when it opens
one of those ideas (a surprising word, a tension, an unusual order of events, a link to what came
before). Point the reader to it and ask what they make of it.

- Usually one short framing sentence in your own words, then the question: "Mark starts his
  gospel with John in the wilderness, not with Jesus's birth. Why do you think he begins here?"
- Never ask the reader to repeat what the text says: no "What does verse 6 say…", "What two
  things…", "How does John describe…", "Who…", "Where…", or any question a reader answers by
  copying words from the passage.
- Aim at a key idea (`targets`: the idea IDs) without giving it away. The framing may name the
  detail; it must not state the point the commentators draw from it.
- Answerable by thinking about the passage alone, with no commentary in front of the reader.
- Open-ended, one question, ending with "?". At most 30 words in all; most good questions need
  15 to 25 (see "Say it short").
- Not leading, and assuming no one tradition's doctrine.
- Quoting: if you put a word or phrase in quotation marks, use single quotation marks ('like
  this'), never double ones. A double quotation mark ends the answer's text field early.
- Doesn't quote wording found in only one of the two translations. Describe it, or use words both
  share. To point to a verse, write `{{ref:C:V}}` or `{{ref:C:V-V}}`.

## Say it short

A question over the limit is dropped, however good it is. Keep the meaning and cut the words:
- Framing sentence: at most about 15 words. Name the detail plainly; don't retell the scene.
  Drop openers such as "In this passage", "Mark tells us that" or "It's worth noticing that".
- Question: at most about 15 words, and one question only. Don't add a second clause ("and what
  does that say about…").
- Point to the detail rather than describing it in full: "the wild animals", "the roof", "the
  word 'immediately'".
- Concrete words over abstract ones: "Why might he leave?" over "What might the significance of
  his departure be?"
- Vary how questions end. Not every question should end "What does that suggest about…?"; also
  use "Why might…?", "What do you make of…?", "How might…?", "What would it mean…?".

Examples (Mark 1), long and then shorter with the same meaning:
- 28 words: "Jesus goes from being driven into the wilderness straight into proclaiming that the
  kingdom of God is near. What does that sequence suggest about how his mission begins?"
  17 words: "Jesus goes straight from the wilderness to announcing God's kingdom. What do you
  make of that order?"
- 28 words: "Jesus told the healed man to go to the priest and follow the process Moses required.
  Why might that official step have mattered beyond simply following the rules?"
  19 words: "Jesus sends the healed man to the priest, as Moses required. Why might that step
  matter beyond the rule?"
- 30 words: "Jesus refuses popularity in one place to bring his message elsewhere. Where do you see
  that tension between depth in one place and breadth across many still playing out today?"
  20 words: "Jesus leaves a town that wants him for towns that haven't heard him. What does that
  say about his purpose?"

## Order

The reader meets one question at a time. The first comes on their first read; each next one a
week later, when they come back to the section. So:
1. First, the question that opens the passage's main idea, usually about the passage as a whole.
2. Then questions on the other key ideas, each aimed at a different idea than the ones before it.
3. A question connecting the passage to the rest of Scripture or to the reader's own life fits
   well later in the list.

Questions are not ranked by difficulty: every one should be worth thinking about on its own.

## What to record

- `type`: `meaning` (what the passage, or something in it, means), `application` (connecting it
  to the rest of Scripture or to life), or `noticing` (points at a detail and asks what the
  reader makes of it; use only when the detail opens a key idea).
- `verseStart`, `verseEnd`: the verses the question is about, inside the section. Use the whole
  section for a question about the passage as a whole.
