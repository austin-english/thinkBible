# Task: guiding questions for one section

You are given one section of Scripture in two translations (BSB and KJV), each commentator's
notes on it, and the key ideas drawn from those notes, each with its ID. Write up to 8 candidate
questions, in the order a reader should meet them. A grader scores every one and the three best
are kept, in your order, so give real options: different ideas and different ways in, each one a
question you would be glad to see kept. Follow the question guide below.

## What a question is for

ThinkBible helps readers uncover what lies beneath the surface of a passage: the meanings a
casual reading misses. The key ideas are those meanings. A question starts from something
concrete the reader can see in the passage (a contrast, a choice someone makes, an order of
events, a word that carries weight) and asks what it reveals, so the reader starts toward the
meaning on their own. The commentators' reply then takes them the rest of the way. A question is
never a reading quiz, and never asks the reader to agree with an answer it has already given.

## The commentators' notes are for you, not the reader

The reader has only the passage in front of them. Use the notes to find the meanings worth
uncovering and the concrete handle in the text that leads toward each one. Never put the
commentators' conclusions into the question.

## Rules

- **Aim beneath the surface.** Aim at a key idea: what the passage shows about God, about Jesus,
  or about following him. Not at a detail for its own sake.
- **Approachable.** A thoughtful reader can make a real start from the passage and what the
  question tells them. When they need one plain fact to get going, state it briefly ("Lepers had
  to live apart from others"). The full depth may arrive in the reply; that is the point.
- **One concrete handle, one simple ask.** Name one thing the reader can see, then ask one short
  question about what it reveals. Not "these two things", not "the passage as a whole", not
  "what picture do these details give".
- **Stands alone.** Readers meet each question on its own, a week apart. Name the people ("Peter's
  mother-in-law", "James and John"), never "her", "the other pair" or "the question above".
- **Plain words.** Ask it the way a friend reading alongside would ask it out loud: who acts,
  ordinary verbs, one clause. Avoid abstract nouns that hide who did what ("a true confession",
  "the affirmation", "the language of teaching"), and these stock endings:
  "Why might that detail be worth noticing?", "Why might he describe it in those particular
  words?", "What does that sequence say about…?", "What connects these two things?",
  "Why might that matter for what comes next?".
- **Doesn't give the meaning away.** The opening sentence says what happens; the question asks
  what it reveals. If the opening sentence already states the meaning ("…steps forward with the
  same message. What does that say about how God's work moves forward?"), the reader has nothing
  left to find.
- Never ask the reader to repeat what the text says ("What does verse 6 say…", "What two
  things…", "Who…", "Where…").
- Open-ended, one question, ending with "?". At most 30 words in all; most good questions need 15
  to 25.
- Not leading toward one tradition's doctrine.
- Quoting: if you put a word or phrase in quotation marks, use single quotation marks ('like
  this'), never double ones. A double quotation mark ends the answer's text field early.
- Doesn't quote wording found in only one of the two translations. Describe it, or use words both
  share. To point to a verse, write `{{ref:C:V}}` or `{{ref:C:V-V}}`.

## The owner's examples

The question guide below has the questions the owner loved. Match their shape: one concrete thing
from the passage, then a short, plain question about what it reveals.

## Questions the owner rejected, and why

- Too vague: "Jesus teaches and then casts out an unclean spirit in the same synagogue, and the
  crowd responds to both with the same amazement. What connects these two things?"
- Too vague: "Mark says Jesus was with the wild animals and that angels served him. What picture
  of those forty days do the two details give together?"
- Doesn't stand alone, clunky: "James and John leave not only their nets but their father. What
  does that detail add to what the other pair's response already showed?" (Who is the other
  pair?)
- Gives the meaning away: "John is arrested, and Jesus steps forward with the same message. What
  does that sequence say about how God's work moves forward?"
- Stilted: "Jesus tells Simon and Andrew he will make them 'fishers of men.' Why might he describe
  the work ahead in those particular words?" (Better: "What does the image of fishing add to what
  following him will mean?")
- Unclear who: "After Jesus heals her, Peter's mother-in-law gets up and immediately serves them.
  What does that response say about what a full healing looks like?"

## Order

The reader meets one question at a time. The first comes on their first read; each next one a
week later, when they come back to the section. Put first the question that opens the passage's
central meaning; then questions on its other key ideas, each aimed at a different idea than the
ones before it.

## What to record

- `type`: `meaning` (what the passage, or something in it, reveals), `application` (connecting it
  to the rest of Scripture or to life), or `noticing` (points at a detail and asks what it
  reveals; use only when the detail opens a key idea).
- `verseStart`, `verseEnd`: the verses the question is about, inside the section.
