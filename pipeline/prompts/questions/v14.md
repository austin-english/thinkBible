# Task: questions that uncover what lies beneath a passage

You are given one section of Scripture in two translations (BSB and KJV), each commentator's
notes on it, and the key ideas drawn from those notes, each with its ID. Write up to 8 candidate
questions, in the order a reader should meet them. A grader scores every one and the three best
are kept, so give real options: different key ideas and different ways in.

## What ThinkBible is for

A reader has just read this passage. Most of what it means lies beneath the surface: why someone
chose what they did, what a reaction shows, why the story is told in this order, what a detail
reveals about God or about following Jesus. A casual reader passes right over it. The key ideas
are those meanings, drawn from the commentators.

Your question is the moment the reader stops and wonders. It points to something they read past
and asks about it, so they start uncovering the meaning on their own. They answer in a sentence or
two, and then the commentators' reply shows them what lies underneath. A question that only checks
that they read the passage, or tells them the answer, wastes that moment.

## How to write one

For each key idea:
1. Find the clue: the thing in the passage that hints at the meaning but that a reader would pass
   over (a choice someone makes, a reaction, an odd order of events, a word that carries weight).
2. Point to it in one plain sentence that says what happens, not what it means.
3. Ask what a curious friend reading alongside would ask about it: why, or what it shows. Keep it
   short and simple.

If the question makes a thoughtful reader stop and wonder about something real, it is good. Write
the way you would talk to that reader, not the way you would write an essay.

The commentators' notes are for you: they show which meanings are worth uncovering and where the
clues are. The reader has not seen them, so never mention the commentators, and never put their
conclusions into the question.

## When a key idea points to Jesus

In the Old Testament a key idea may say that the passage points to Jesus: a prophecy fulfilled in
him, or a picture of him. Let the reader make that connection, and then go past it. "Who is this
about?" is a quiz with a one-word answer. Point to the details that fit, and ask what the
connection opens up: what it shows about his death or his work, why it matters that it was
foretold, what the picture says about how he saves. You may name Jesus: readers know him, and
what they discover is how this passage fits him. For example: "The lamb dies, and every house
with its blood on the door is passed over that night. Why might Jesus' first followers have kept
coming back to this night?"

## A few firm limits

- One question, ending with "?". At most 30 words; most good questions need 15 to 25.
- Open-ended: never answerable with yes or no, and never with one obvious word.
- Names its people ("Peter's mother-in-law", "James and John"): readers see each question on its
  own, a week apart.
- No verse numbers or references: the app shows which verses a question is about.
- Doesn't quote wording found in only one translation; describe it instead. If you put a word in
  quotation marks, use single ones ('like this'), never double ones, which end the text field.
- Assumes no one church tradition's doctrine where Christian traditions differ. Pointing to Jesus
  where a key idea does (below) is not one tradition's doctrine.

## Questions the owner rejected, and why

- Only checks reading: "What does verse 6 tell us about John's clothing and diet?"
- Tells the answer: "John is arrested, and Jesus steps forward with the same message. What does
  that sequence say about how God's work moves forward?"
- Too vague to wonder about: "Jesus teaches and then casts out an unclean spirit in the same
  synagogue, and the crowd responds to both with the same amazement. What connects these two
  things?"
- Answers itself: "John is imprisoned, and Jesus immediately steps out preaching the same message.
  What does that say about what can and cannot stop the gospel?" (Answer: nothing can.)
- Worded like an essay: "The crowd in verse 27 responds to the exorcism by asking about the
  teaching. What does that response suggest they understood the miracle to be doing?" Said
  simply: "The crowd sees a demon driven out and starts talking about Jesus' teaching. Why might a
  miracle turn their attention to what he taught?"

The questions the owner loved are in the question guide below.

## Order, and the first question

The reader meets one question at a time: the first on their first read, each next one a week
later. The first question matters most. It is what a reader meets straight after reading, and it
decides whether they come back.

One key idea is marked CENTRAL MEANING: what the passage as a whole is about. Write your first
two or three candidates on it, each from a different clue or way in, so the grader has a real
choice; the best of them is asked first. Each follows everything above: it points to a clue and
asks about it, and never names or hints at the meaning. Then write questions on the other key
ideas.

## What to record

- `type`: `meaning` (what something in the passage reveals), `application` (connecting it to the
  rest of Scripture or to life), or `noticing` (points at a detail and asks what it reveals).
- `verseStart`, `verseEnd`: the verses the question is about, inside the section.
