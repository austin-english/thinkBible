# Task: key ideas, context and "views differ" for one section

You are given one section of Scripture in two translations (BSB and KJV) and each commentator's
notes on it. Some notes are labelled "Parallel passage note": that commentator wrote their note on
a parallel passage (often in another Gospel) and points readers to it from here. Treat those
notes as the commentator's comment on this section's passage.

A commentator's note often covers more verses than this section; its label then says that it
runs past the section and which verses the section is. Work only from what the notes say about
this section's verses. The rest tells you what comes before and after, and nothing more: every
key idea, the context note and the views-differ note must be about something in this section's
verses, and an idea's `verseStart` and `verseEnd` must be the verses it is really about.

## Key ideas (1 to 5) and background (0 to 2)

ThinkBible exists to help readers uncover what lies beneath the surface of a passage: the
meanings a casual reading misses, which the commentators bring out. A key idea is one of those
meanings. Readers answer a question first and then see these ideas, so each one should be
something they are glad to discover.

Each idea has a `kind`:
- `key`: a deeper meaning the commentators draw from this passage: what it shows about God,
  about Jesus, about sin and salvation, or about following him, including a link to the rest of
  Scripture that the commentator makes (Jesus was tempted so that he can help those who are
  tempted). The passage points toward it, though a reader may not see it on a first read.
  Questions aim at these, and the app tells readers which ones they caught. Give 1 to 5.
- `background`: a fact worth knowing that is not itself a meaning: only another Gospel records
  it ("only Mark records…"), what the original word is ("Mark's word is sharper than…"), a custom,
  a date, an idiom. Readers see these as "More from the commentators" and are never told they
  missed them. Give 0 to 2, only when genuinely useful.

Test each idea: is it something a reader would be glad to discover about God or the life of
faith? Then it is key. Is it a fact that helps picture the scene? Then it is background. A key
idea says why something matters; it never just restates what happens in the story ("Peter's
mother-in-law served them" is not an idea; what her serving shows is). A point one commentator
makes against the others' reading belongs in `viewsDiffer`, not in an idea.

- Restate only what the commentators say. Do not add interpretations, facts, or cross-references
  they don't give, and do not choose between them where they disagree. Neutral means not taking
  sides where the commentators disagree with each other, or where Christian traditions differ
  (Reformed, Wesleyan, Catholic and others).
- Don't name the commentators in the title or body ("Henry notes…", "all three agree…"). The app
  shows the sources next to each idea, and only the ones your quotes support.
- `title`: the point itself, at most 8 words.
- `body`: one or two sentences, at most 30 words: the meaning, and why it matters.
- Write plainly, as a thoughtful friend would explain it: short sentences, everyday words, no
  dashes, no superlatives or flourishes, no 18th- and 19th-century phrasing.
- Quoting: if you put a word or phrase in quotation marks, use single quotation marks ('like
  this').
- Don't quote either translation's wording. Describe what the verses say in your own words. To
  point to a verse, write a placeholder: `{{ref:C:V}}` for one verse or `{{ref:C:V-V}}` for a
  range, using this section's chapter and verse numbers.
- `sources`: every commentator who makes this point, by id: `matthew-henry`,
  `jamieson-fausset-brown`, `adam-clarke`. When two or three make the same point, list them all
  on one idea rather than writing near-duplicate ideas.
- `evidence`: for every commentator in `sources`, one quote of 8 to 30 words copied exactly, word
  for word, from that commentator's notes above. The quote must show that this commentator makes
  this point. A source you can't quote must be left out.
  Readers see these quotes under "In their words", so choose words that read well on their own:
  a whole sentence or clause in which the commentator makes the point. Not a Bible verse the
  commentator quotes, not Greek or Hebrew, not a list of references, and not a fragment that
  starts or stops mid-thought. Henry: "The silencing of Christ's ministers shall not be the
  suppressing of Christ's gospel" is the kind of quote to look for.
- `verseStart` and `verseEnd`: the verses this idea is about, inside this section. Use the whole
  section when the point is about the passage as a whole.
- Order: the central meaning first (below), then the most important and best-supported ideas.
  Give fewer ideas rather than weak ones, but don't leave out a point two or more commentators
  make.

## Central meaning (`centralIdea`)

Give the number (1 for the first) of the key idea that is the passage's central meaning: what the
passage as a whole is about, the one meaning a reader should take away if they took away only
one. The reader's first question opens it, so choose carefully:

- Look for what the commentators treat as the point of the whole passage: the summary they give
  before going verse by verse, and the point they keep coming back to.
- A point two or three commentators make is strong support, but sharing a point is not enough on
  its own. A detail they all mention (a place, a custom) is not the central meaning.
- Not a striking detail from one verse, unless the whole passage turns on that verse.
- Always a key idea, never background. Write its title and body so they speak for the whole
  passage.

## Christ in the Old Testament

In the Old Testament the commentators often read a passage as foretelling Jesus (a prophecy
fulfilled in him) or as a picture of him (a person, event or object that points to him), and they
often name the New Testament passages that say so. When they make that reading in their notes on
this section, it is a key idea, and usually the central meaning. Say it plainly: name Jesus, say
what the passage shows about him and his work, and give the New Testament link they give, in
words (placeholders are only for this section's verses). For example, if the commentators read the
Passover lamb, whose blood on the door spared each household, as a picture of Jesus, and recall
that Paul calls Christ our Passover, the key idea says so and why it matters.

- This is common ground for Christian readers, not one tradition's view, when the commentators
  who make it are not contradicted by another of them. Don't hedge it ("the servant", "the figure
  in this passage") when they name Jesus.
- A reading the commentators mention only to answer it (that the passage is about someone else)
  may go in `viewsDiffer`, but it doesn't make the key idea hedge.
- When one commentator makes the link and another rejects it, keep the key idea to what they
  share and describe the difference in `viewsDiffer`.
- Never add a link the commentators don't make.

## Context (`context`)

One or two short sentences, at most 35 words in all, about this section: where and when it takes
place, who is present, and how it follows from what just happened (the previous section's summary
is given above). Readers meet the book's own introduction (who wrote it, to whom, and why) once,
before its first passage, so never repeat that here, and never say why the book was written. Use
what the commentators say about this passage.

## Views differ (`viewsDiffer`)

Where the commentators, or the traditions they mention, understand this passage differently,
describe the difference neutrally in at most 55 words, without taking a side. A reading they
mention only to answer it may be described here too. Only
differences in what this passage means count. Leave out differences between manuscripts or
translations (those are handled separately), and differences about other passages, including how
another Gospel tells the same event. Otherwise null.

## Say it short

The word limits leave room to spare; text over them is dropped. Lead with the point, one idea per
sentence, in plain, concrete words.

## No prompt (`noPrompt`)

Set `noPrompt` to true only when the commentators give nothing a reader could be asked about
from the passage itself, for example a bare list of names they don't discuss. Decide this from
the commentary, never from the kind of passage: genealogies, lists and laws still get key ideas
wherever the commentators discuss them. When true, give a short `noPromptReason`; key ideas are
still welcome if the commentators make any point.
