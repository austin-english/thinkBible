# Task: key ideas, context and "views differ" for one section

You are given one section of Scripture in two translations (BSB and KJV) and each commentator's
notes on it. Some notes are labelled "Parallel passage note": that commentator wrote their note on
a parallel passage (often in another Gospel) and points readers to it from here. Treat those
notes as the commentator's comment on this section's passage.

## Key ideas (1 to 5) and background (0 to 2)

A key idea is a point the commentators make about this passage that helps a reader understand it,
and that a careful reader could come to see by thinking about the passage itself. Readers answer a
question first and then see these ideas, so each one should teach something.

Each idea has a `kind`:
- `key`: a careful reader could see it in this passage, in either translation, with no other
  book open. Questions aim at these, and the app tells readers which ones they caught. Give 1 to
  5.
- `background`: worth knowing, but it needs something the passage doesn't show: another Gospel
  ("only Mark records…"), the original language ("Mark's word is sharper than…"), history, or
  other Scripture the passage doesn't quote by name. Readers see these as "More from the
  commentators" and are never told they missed them. Give 0 to 2, only when genuinely useful.

Test each idea: could a thoughtful reader with only this passage in front of them arrive at it?
If not, it is background. A point one commentator makes against the others' reading belongs in
`viewsDiffer`, not in an idea.

- Restate only what the commentators say. Do not add interpretations, facts, or cross-references
  they don't give, and do not choose between them where they disagree.
- Don't name the commentators in the title or body ("Henry notes…", "all three agree…"). The app
  shows the sources next to each idea, and only the ones your quotes support.
- `title`: the point itself, at most 8 words.
- `body`: one or two sentences, at most 30 words: what the point is and why it matters for
  understanding the passage.
- Write plainly, as a thoughtful friend would explain it: short sentences, everyday words, no
  dashes, no superlatives or flourishes, no 18th- and 19th-century phrasing.
- Quoting: if you put a word or phrase in quotation marks, use single quotation marks ('like
  this'), never double ones. A double quotation mark ends the answer's text field early.
- Don't quote either translation's wording. Describe what the verses say in your own words. To
  point to a verse, write a placeholder: `{{ref:C:V}}` for one verse or `{{ref:C:V-V}}` for a
  range, using this section's chapter and verse numbers.
- `sources`: every commentator who makes this point, by id: `matthew-henry`,
  `jamieson-fausset-brown`, `adam-clarke`. When two or three make the same point, list them all
  on one idea rather than writing near-duplicate ideas.
- `evidence`: for every commentator in `sources`, one quote of 8 to 30 words copied exactly, word
  for word, from that commentator's notes above. The quote must show that this commentator makes
  this point. A source you can't quote must be left out.
  Readers see these quotes under "In their words", so choose words that read well on their own:
  a whole sentence or clause in which the commentator makes the point. Not a Bible verse the
  commentator quotes, not Greek or Hebrew, not a list of references, and not a fragment that
  starts or stops mid-thought. Henry: "The silencing of Christ's ministers shall not be the
  suppressing of Christ's gospel" is the kind of quote to look for.
- `verseStart` and `verseEnd`: the verses this idea is about, inside this section. Use the whole
  section when the point is about the passage as a whole.
- Order: the most important and best-supported ideas first. Give fewer ideas rather than weak
  ones, but don't leave out a point two or more commentators make.

## Context (`context`)

Two short sentences, at most 35 words in all: who wrote this book, to whom, and why, as it bears
on this section. Use what the commentators say; where they say nothing on it, keep to what is
common ground.

## Views differ (`viewsDiffer`)

Where the commentators, or the traditions they mention, understand this passage differently,
describe the difference neutrally in at most 55 words, without taking a side. Only
differences in what this passage means count. Leave out differences between manuscripts or
translations (those are handled separately), and differences about other passages, including how
another Gospel tells the same event. Otherwise null.

## Say it short

The word limits leave room to spare; text over them is dropped, so stay well inside. Cut words,
never meaning:
- Lead with the point. Drop openers such as "This passage shows that", "It is significant that"
  or "Mark tells us that".
- One idea per sentence. Cut a clause that restates the title or retells the verse.
- Plain, concrete words over abstract ones ("shows" over "serves to demonstrate").

Examples (Mark 1), long and then shorter with the same meaning:
- Context, 42 words: "Mark's Gospel, widely understood as drawing on Peter's eyewitness
  testimony, was written for a Gentile audience. This section shows Jesus beginning his public
  work in Capernaum, demonstrating authority in both teaching and over evil spirits, establishing
  the shape of his whole ministry."
  Better, 26 words: "Mark wrote for Gentile believers, likely drawing on Peter's memories. Here Jesus
  begins his work in Capernaum, showing authority in what he teaches and over evil."
- Key idea body, 39 words: "The commentators see leprosy as a vivid picture of sin: it spreads
  quietly, defiles everything it touches, separates a person from the community and from worship,
  and could not be cured by any human remedy, only by God's power."
  Better, 23 words: "Leprosy pictures sin: it spreads, defiles, and cuts a person off from God's
  people. Only God could cure it, never a human remedy."

## No prompt (`noPrompt`)

Set `noPrompt` to true only when the commentators give nothing a reader could be asked about
from the passage itself, for example a bare list of names they don't discuss. Decide this from
the commentary, never from the kind of passage: genealogies, lists and laws still get key ideas
wherever the commentators discuss them. When true, give a short `noPromptReason`; key ideas are
still welcome if the commentators make any point.
