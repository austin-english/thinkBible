# Question guide

The commentary decides what is worth asking about; the passage is what the reader answers from.
A question guides the reader into what the passage means. It is never a quiz on what they read.
These questions set ThinkBible's voice.

## Quality rules

- Big picture: aim at the main ideas the commentators draw from the passage. A detail is worth
  asking about when it opens one of them, not for its own sake.
- Guiding, not testing: start from something significant in the passage (a surprising detail, a
  tension, an unusual order, a word that carries weight) and ask what the reader makes of it.
  Never ask the reader to repeat what the text says.
- Open-ended: never a yes-or-no question.
- Short: usually one framing sentence and one question.
- Answerable by thinking about the passage itself.
- Not leading: the framing may point at the detail, but never states the point the commentators
  draw from it.
- No assumptions about one tradition's doctrine. On contested passages keep the question open
  ("What does this passage say about God's choosing?").
- Works in either translation: describe what the verses say rather than quoting wording only one
  translation uses ("Holy Spirit" in one, "Holy Ghost" in the other).
- Shaped by the passage: in a story ask why people act as they do; in a letter about the
  argument; in poetry about the images; in prophecy about the promise or warning. In genealogies,
  lists and laws, ask about the verses the commentators single out.
- Warm and curious, as a friend reading alongside.

## A section's questions

Up to three, one per key idea at most, in the order the reader meets them: the first on the first
read, each next one a week after the reader answers the one before. There are no levels and no
ranking by difficulty. The first opens the passage's main idea; later ones take up other ideas,
and may connect the passage to the rest of Scripture or to the reader's life.

## Avoid: quiz questions

These test whether the reader read the verses rather than help them understand them:
- What two things does Mark say about Jesus in the very first sentence of his gospel?
- What does verse 6 tell us about John's clothing and diet?
- How does John describe the One who is coming after him?
- What three things happen as Jesus comes up out of the water?

## Examples (Mark 1)

From the Mark 1 designs and the first pipeline runs. Where a translation's wording differs, the
BSB form is shown with the KJV one after it.

The passage as a whole
1. Mark starts his gospel with John in the wilderness, not with Jesus's birth. Why do you think he
   begins here? (1:1-8)
2. Mark opens with prophecy, then a wilderness preacher, then a baptism. What does this sequence
   say about how God's plans unfold? (1:1-11)
3. The passage moves from wilderness and testing to a public announcement that the kingdom is
   near. What does that arc suggest about how Jesus' mission begins? (1:12-15)

An idea opened by a detail
4. Mark names Jesus 'Son of God' before telling a single story. Why might that opening label
   matter for everything that follows? (1:1)
5. Mark quotes the prophets before he tells us anything about John. What does that say about
   John's role? (1:2-4)
6. John baptizes with water, but says Jesus will baptize with the Holy Spirit. What difference is
   he pointing to? (1:7-8)
   KJV: … but says Jesus will baptize with the Holy Ghost. …
7. Right after his baptism, the Spirit drives Jesus into the wilderness. What do you make of that
   immediate connection? (1:12)
8. God says he is pleased with Jesus before Jesus has done any public ministry. Why do you think
   that matters? (1:11)

Beyond the passage
9. Forty days in the wilderness may remind readers of Israel's forty years. What connection do you
   see? (1:12-13)
10. Simon and Andrew leave their nets at once to follow Jesus. What might leaving your nets look
    like for you? (1:16-18)
